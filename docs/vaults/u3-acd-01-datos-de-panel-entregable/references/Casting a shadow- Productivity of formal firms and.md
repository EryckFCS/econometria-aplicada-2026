Abstract
Informality is a salient feature of most developing econo-
mies. Two important concerns arise from informality. First, 
informal firms tend to be less productive than formal firms. 
Second, the competition from informal firms can weigh on 
the productivity of firms in the formal sector. These chal-
lenges can lower the aggregate productivity and thereby 
retard overall development in countries where the informal 
sector is pervasive. Our analysis provides robust evidence 
on both issues using firm-level survey data for a large cross 
section of countries. We also examine the extent to which 
firm characteristics and business environment quality shape 
the productivity gap between formal and informal firms as 
well as the impact of informal competition on the productiv-
ity of formal sector firms. The results show that, on average, 
the labor productivity of informal firms is about one-fourth 
that of formal firms. Moreover, competition from infor-
mal firms lowers the labor productivity of formal firms by 
20%–24%. In line with the “parasite” view of informality, 
the negative impact of informal competition on the produc-
tivity of the formal sector firms is more pronounced when 
the business environment is less conducive to operating for-
mally due to high corruption, burdensome regulations, and 
weak institutions.

1 | INTRODUCTION
Understanding informality and its economic implications is of paramount importance for poverty-re-
ducing and welfare-enhancing policies. Informal businesses absorb nearly 70% of the labor force in 
emerging markets and developing economies (ILO, 2018). Despite its potential contribution to the 
economy, there is a concern that the informal sector undermines aggregate productivity. This can hap-
pen for at least two reasons. First, informal firms are highly inefficient vis-à-vis formal sector firms, 
which lowers aggregate productivity. Second, the competition from informal firms can weigh on the 
productivity of firms in the formal sector.
This paper uses firm-level survey data collected by the World Bank to analyze the aforementioned 
concerns of informality. Specifically, we estimate the difference in labor productivity between formal 
and informal firms (henceforth, productivity gap) and the impact of competition from informal firms 
(henceforth, informal competition) on the labor productivity of the formal firms. We contribute to the 
literature in the following ways.
First, we provide new evidence on the labor productivity gap and the impact of informal compe-
tition (on formal firms’ labor productivity) using firm-level survey data for a large cross section of 
mostly developing countries.
Second, we pay due attention to potential endogeneity concerns in estimating the impact of infor-
mal competition. To this end, we proxy the informal competition faced by a given formal firm by the 
level of informal competition experienced by all other formal firms in the same location–size–industry 
category. This strategy helps substantially mitigate endogeneity concerns. Additional endogeneity 
checks are undertaken by testing some implications of the “parasite” view of informality. These en-
dogeneity checks are motivated by the study of Distinguin, Rugemintwari, and Tacneng (2016), among 
others, and control for the heterogeneous effects of informal competition on the productivity of the 
formal firms. For the productivity gap analysis, our approach is more modest due to data limitations.
Third, the World Bank’s Enterprise Surveys (ES) we use are conducted using a harmonized sam-
pling methodology and questionnaire that facilitate cross-country comparability while allowing to 
control for heterogeneity across data points within a country. Previous studies (reviewed in Section 2) 
using microdata are largely restricted to a single country or a narrow set of countries, and it is not clear 
if the results of these studies hold more generally.
Fourth, we explore the heterogeneity in the size of the productivity gap and the impact of informal 
competition. As discussed in detail later in this paper, informality may impact countries and firms dif-
ferently. For instance, informal firms operating at the fringes of the formal–informal divide are likely 
to enjoy a greater “unfair” advantage over formal firms when the cost of operating formally versus 
informally is higher and the benefits lower. This is more likely to be the case when the regulatory 
burden on formal firms is greater, corruption is higher, and enforcement of rules and laws is weaker. 
Similarly, the productivity gap may vary if, for example, informal firms are less capable of benefiting 
than the formal firms from factors that boost productivity such as managerial experience and location. 
Exploring heterogeneity is important from both academic and policy-making standpoints. One rea-
son is that the size of the informal sector has remained large in many countries despite the economic 
progress and several formalization programs. The heterogeneity analysis allows us to identify which 
factors exacerbate the harmful effects of informality and thereby helps design more targeted policy 
responses.
Our results show that the labor productivity of informal firms is on average about one-fourth that 
of formal firms (Figure 1). Firm characteristics such as higher age and location in the capital city that 
tend to boost labor productivity are found to benefit the formal firms more than the informal firms.

Moreover, we find that the labor productivity of formal firms facing informal competition is on

for the informal competition results, as detailed data permit a more rigorous robustness analysis.
2 | LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 | Productivity difference between formal and informal firms
The productivity gap between formal and informal firms has been discussed in several papers 
(Benjamin & Mbaye, 2012; Farrell, 2004; Gelb, Mengistae, Ramachandran, & Shah, 2009; La Porta 
& Shleifer, 2008, 2014; Perry et al., 2007). As pointed out by Farrell (2004, p. 30), firms in the infor-
mal sector tend to “become trapped in a self-reinforcing dynamic that confines them to subscale, inef-
ficient, low-productivity work,” leading to a productivity gap across the formal–informal divide. This 
may happen due to important differences between informal and formal firms such as unequal access to 
public services (Steel & Snodgrass, 2008), difference in the business environment quality (Gelb et al.,

A range of productivity gap estimates are found in the literature, partly because studies use various 
samples and different definitions of informality. Using a sample of firms from seven Latin American 
and Caribbean countries, Perry et al. (2007) find that the labor productivity of firms that started their 
operations without being formally registered (referred to as informal firms) is about 29% lower than 
the productivity of firms that have always operated formally. La Porta and Shleifer (2008) use, like 
us, the World Bank’s ES data for a cross section of developing countries. They find that in the median 
sample country, informal (unregistered) firms have only 15% of the value added per worker of formal 
(registered) firms. The authors suggest that the observed gap stems from the smaller size of the in-
formal firms compared to formal firms and differences in input use, especially management quality. 
Benjamin and Mbaye (2012) focus on the three capital cities of Benin, Burkina Faso, and Senegal. The 
authors use a multifaceted definition of informality based on the business’ activity size, registration 
status, bank credit availability, workplace mobility, and financial reporting. They confirm that labor 
productivity is much lower among informal firms than the formal firms, largely due to smaller-sized 
informal firms in the sample. Echevin and Murtin (2009) use matched employer–employee data col-
lected by World Bank and Afristat in 2003 for Senegal. They corroborate that labor productivity is 
much lower for informal (unregistered) firms than the formal (registered) firms. Surveying existing 
studies around the world, Farrell (2004) notes that informal businesses operate at just half the average 
productivity level of formal companies in the same sector.

2.2 | Impact of informal competition on the productivity of formal 
sector firms
Another strand of the literature investigates how competition from informal firms affects the produc-
tivity and performance of firms in the formal sector. The two prominent perspectives are the “para-
site” view and the “dual economy” view (La Porta & Shleifer, 2008, 2014). The “dual economy” view 
asserts that formal and informal firms are fundamentally different. Informal labor is a by-product of 
poverty and failure to find good jobs in the formal sector. Informal businesses are small, inefficient, 
of subsistence nature, and their productivity is too low to allow them to compete and survive in the 
formal sector. Thus, there is no spillover from informal to formal sector (La Porta & Shleifer, 2008, 
2014; Rothenberg et al., 2016).
In contrast, the “parasite” view considers informal firms as capable of competing against formal 
firms. Informality arises from a rational decision of firms to avoid costly regulations and taxes—the 
rational exit model (De Mel, McKenzie, &Woodruff, 2011; Maloney, 2004). Such informal firms 
operate on the fringes of the formal–informal divide, providing stiff competition to the formal sector 
firms. In general, on a level-playing field, competition can lead to higher productivity through in-
dustry rationalization and improved efficiency (Djankov & Murrell, 2002). However, informal firms 
do not pay taxes and do not comply with costly regulations, whereas formal firms must shoulder the 
cost of compliance. This “unfair” advantage allows informal firms to charge lower prices and “steal” 
market share from the formal firms. Thus, formal firms’ production declines with consequent loss of 
scale-related economies. Lower prices impinge on the profitability of the formal firms, reducing their

The empirical evidence on the impact of informal competition on the productivity of formal firms 
is rare. In an early attempt, Tokman (1978) finds that in the city of Santiago, informal foodstuff com-
mercial establishments can successfully compete with formal sector counterparts (modern supermar-
kets). However, the effect of informal competition on the productivity of formal establishments is not 
discussed. Gonzalez and Lamanna (2007) analyze firm-level survey data on formal manufacturing 
firms collected by the World Bank’s ES for 14 countries in Latin America. Their findings suggest that 
about 40% of the firms in the region face significant informal competition, with sizeable variation 
across industries and firm sizes. However, the study does not offer any direct evidence on the impact 
of informal competition on formal firms’ productivity. Using ES data for Nicaragua, Pisani (2015) 
explores the firm characteristics that determine the likelihood of formal firms to face informal compe-
tition. Mendi and Costamagna (2017) use data from ES to estimate the impact of informal competition 
on formal sector firms’ innovation. Again, these studies do not assess the direct impact of informal 
competition on formal firms’ productivity.
La Porta and Shleifer (2008) use ES data to examine the expected effects of informality on the 
formal sector firms. However, the authors do not investigate the impact on productivity. Instead, they 
analyze surveyed formal firms’ responses to whether “anticompetitive and informal” practices are 
an obstacle for their business. The authors acknowledge that the question is noisy because anticom-
petitive practices can result from the behavior of the formal sector firms. Firms’ view of informality 
as an obstacle may also be driven by the perceived impact of the informal activity on total revenues 
or profits rather than on productivity. Moreover, La Porta and Shleifer (2008) use old data collected 
between 2002 and 2007.
Other studies highlight the negative effects of informality on the formal sector and the overall 
economic development. Perry et al. (2007) argue that informality can have negative effects on formal 
firms’ investment and innovation decisions because it reduces their market share and profitability. 
However, they do not provide any empirical evidence to support this claim. Rozo and Winkler (2019) 
find that, in Colombia, an increase in internally displaced persons expands the informal sector, which 
in turn adversely affects the total output of the formal sector firms. Loayza (1996) suggests that a 
large informal economy may lower tax revenues and increase public service congestion. The nega-
tive effects of congestion, however, fall on all firms and therefore cannot be linked to the informal 
competition.
3 | DATA DESCRIPTION AND MAIN VARIABLES
3.1 | Data and empirical strategy
We define informality as the lack of formal registration of firms. Simply put, unregistered firms 
belong to the informal sector, whereas formal firms are those that are registered with the relevant 
authorities. The merits and limitations of various definitions of informality are discussed in Benjamin 
and Mbaye (2012).
Our main data source is firm-level survey data collected by the World Bank’s ES on formal or 
registered firms with five or more employees. These surveys are representative of the nonagricul-
tural and nonfinancial registered private economy—excluding firms with fewer than five employees. 
A common sampling methodology—stratified random sampling—and a harmonized questionnaire 
are used in all surveyed countries. These data are complemented with data on informal businesses

3.3 | Main explanatory variables
For the productivity gap analysis, the main explanatory variable is a dummy that equals 1 if the firm 
is informal and 0 otherwise (informal firm).
For the impact of informal competition on the labor productivity of the formal firms, the main ex-
planatory variable is a measure of informal competition faced by formal firms. Specifically, the ES ask 
formal firms whether they compete against informal firms. The variable cannot be used directly in the 
regressions as it is likely to be endogenous to many firm characteristics. In fact, whether a firm faces 
informal competition may itself depend on its level of labor productivity (reverse causality problem).
One solution suggested in the literature is to proxy the informal competition faced by a formal firm 
with the average level of informal competition faced by all other formal firms (except the firm in ques-
tion) in the same location, industry, and size group (henceforth, cell). Reverse causality from a given 
formal firm’s productivity to the informal competition experienced by other formal firms in the cell is 
highly unlikely although the possibility cannot be ruled out completely (see, e.g., Aterido et al., 2011). 
Moreover, since the cell excludes the firm in question, the cell average of informal competition is un-
likely to be correlated with the characteristics of the firm in question. This helps to mitigate the omitted 
variable bias problem. Using the cell average also helps control for potential measurement errors if 
some firms choose not to respond or misstate the informal competition that they experience (Pounov, 
2016). A few examples of studies that use cell averages in the sense described previously include Dollar, 
Hallward-Driemeier, and Mengistae (2006), Fisman and Svensson (2007), De Rosa, Gooroochurn, and 
Görg (2010), Aterido, Hallward-Driemeier, and Pages (2011), and Amin and Soh (2020).
In constructing the cells, a choice needs to be made about location, industry, and size. Cells defined 
at a more disaggregated level are more likely to contain firms that face similar levels of informal com-
petition. However, the number of firms in each cell is reduced, raising concerns about the reliability 
of the cell average. In line with the literature, we define the level of informal competition faced by 
a formal firm as the proportion of all firms in the region–industry–size cell excluding the firm in 
question that reports competing against informal firms. For firm size, we use the ES classification of 
small (fewer than 20 employees), medium (20–99 employees), and large (100 or more employees). For 
region, we use the subnational regions that match the ES strata. For industry, we use the three-digit 
ISIC Rev. 3.1 groupings. Excluding cells with fewer than three firms, we are left with 7,081 region–
industry–size cells.

Our first set of controls includes dummy variables that indicate the country where the firm is 
located (country fixed effects). Thus, differences in overall economic development, financial develop-
ment, and quality of macro-level institutions are purged from our estimation results.
Next, we control for the number of workers (logs) employed at the firm. More workers imply larger 
firms that can lead to higher productivity due to the presence of fixed costs and greater allocative ef-
ficiency (Bartelsman, Haltiwanger, & Scarpetta, 2013; Tybout, 2000). However, diminishing returns 
to labor may prevail as fixed capital and resources are spread out over a larger number of workers. In 
that case, labor productivity may decline as the number of workers increases. Amin and Islam (2015) 
use ES data on informal firms and find that labor productivity among informal firms declines sharply 
with firm size.
The link between productivity and age of the firm has been extensively investigated. Age-related 
effects among surviving firms can be explained by scale economies gained from expansion over time, 
vintage effects due to younger firms employing new and improved technology or equipment, selection 
effects that weed out inefficient firms, and learning by doing (see, e.g., Bahk & Gort, 1993; Jensen, 
Robert, & Stiroh, 2001; Jovanovic, 1982). If formal and informal firms differ systematically in their age, 
it could induce an omitted variable bias in our estimates. Thus, we control for the (log of) age of the firm.

Several studies have shown that female-owned or managed firms tend to be much smaller, less 
profitable, and less efficient than the male-owned or managed firms (Du Rietz & Henrekson, 2000; 
Islam, Gaddis, Palacios-Lopez, & Amin, 2018). This could be due to difficulties that women face in 
obtaining credit and accessing institutions, laws that discriminate against women, and culture that 
tends to discourage women’s work. Thus, we control for a dummy variable equal to 1 if the main de-
cision maker of the firm is a woman and 0 otherwise.
Our last control is for the location of the firm within the country. Capital cities are typically among 
countries’ largest economic centers and, therefore, can offer agglomeration benefits: larger markets, 
better infrastructure to access markets and operate, a larger pool of workers, and greater technology 
spillovers (Rosenthal & Strange, 2004). This is likely to impact labor productivity positively. To guard 
against the implied omitted variable bias problem, we control for a dummy variable equal to 1 if the 
firm is in the capital city of the country and 0 otherwise.
Information on the capital stock used by the firms is not available in informality surveys. Formal 
and informal firms were asked if they use machinery and equipment. However, this is too crude a 
measure of capital stock especially for the formal sector firms. A failure to control for the capital stock 
may lead to omitted variable bias in our empirical estimation of the productivity gap. Therefore, due 
caution is necessary for interpreting our results.
4.2 | Base regression results
Our base regression results for the productivity gap are given in Table 2. These results show that ir-
respective of the controls, labor productivity is lower for informal firms than for formal firms. The 
productivity differential is quantitatively large ranging between − 1.36 and − 1.65 (log points). The 
differential is significant at the 1% level in all the specifications. The most conservative estimate of the 
productivity gap is obtained in the final specification that includes all the controls. According to this 
estimate, the labor productivity of informal firms is lower by about 74% compared to a formal firm.
Regarding the various controls, labor productivity is significantly higher (at the 5% or 1% level) 
for firms with more experienced managers, older firms, firms located in the capital cities, services 
sector firms versus manufacturing, firms with a male versus female top manager, and the relatively 
smaller firms.

5 | LABOR PRODUCTIVITY OF THE FORMAL FIRMS 
THAT COMPETE AGAINST THE INFORMAL FIRMS
In Section 4, we reported evidence of a large labor productivity gap between formal and informal 
firms. Thus, informality undermines aggregate productivity and retards overall economic develop-
ment. This section focuses on the second way in which informality lowers aggregate productivity. 
This happens through the adverse effect of informal competition on the labor productivity of the 
formal sector firms.

The motivation for all the controls has already been discussed in Section 3.1 except for exports,

Wagner, 2007). Therefore, we control for the proportion of firms’ annual sales made abroad (exports).

and replacement value of machines, equipment) is missing due to nonresponse for more than half the 
firms. Therefore, following Amin and Soh (2020) among others, we use a proxy measure of capital 
use that is a dummy variable equal to 1 if the firm spent on the purchase of fixed assets during the past 
year and 0 otherwise. We acknowledge that this is a partial measure of capital use and therefore our 
results could reflect differences in capital use across firms.
The second part of the empirical analysis explores the heterogeneity in the labor productivity and 
informal competition relationship. This is motivated by the “parasite” view of informality suggesting 
that firms chose to go informal not because they cannot compete with formal firms but to avoid taxes 
and other costly regulations. The “unfair” advantage enjoyed by the informal firms hurts the formal 
sector firms. We argue that this “unfair” advantage is greater when the cost of operating formally 
versus informally is higher or the benefits lower. Distinguin et al. (2016) make a similar point in their 
analysis of the impact of informal competition on access to finance for formal SMEs. They find that 
the impact is negative and more so in countries with higher corruption, higher taxes, and weaker rule 
of law. For instance, they argue that a strong rule of law raises the likelihood of informal activity de-
tection; therefore, informal firms are forced to keep their operations small, weakening their capacity 
to affect formal SMEs.
Like Distinguin et al. (2016), we focus on corruption, regulatory burden, and quality of enforce-
ment as factors that exacerbate or mitigate the effects of informal competition. Thus, our testable 
hypothesize is that the negative impact of informal competition on the labor productivity of the formal 
firms should be stronger (more negative) in countries where formal firms face higher corruption, 
greater regulatory burden, and weaker enforcement mechanisms for implementing laws and rules.
The heterogeneity analysis serves two purposes. First, it unveils the factors that exacerbate the im-
pact on informality. This is important for a more targeted policy intervention. Second, the hypothesis 
stated in the previous paragraph assumes that mechanisms specific to the functioning of the informal 
sector are at play. This hypothesis is unlikely to hold if our informal competition variable is a mere 
proxy for other economic development correlates. Thus, testing for the hypothesis serves as a useful 
check against endogeneity concerns.

5.4 | Underreporting of output
Formal firms that face competition from the informal firms may underreport output to reduce the tax 
burden and other compliance costs. Thus, one may argue that underreporting may partly explain the 
productivity difference between formal firms that compete against informal firms and those that do 
not. A few points are noteworthy here.
First, formal firms that underreport sales are also likely to underreport the number of workers. 
Thus, it is not clear if underreporting increases or reduces the observed productivity difference.
Second, we regress the labor productivity of a given firm on informal competition faced by other 
firms in the cell. It is unlikely that informal competition faced by other firms in the cell affects under-
reporting by the firm in question although the possibility cannot be ruled out completely.
Third, we hypothesize that it is more difficult to hide output for firms that export, have foreign 
owners, are in the capital city, and have declared fixed asset purchases during the past year. Indeed, 
firms that underreport sales are also less likely to report capital expansion (purchase of fixed assets). 
Similarly, exporting firms must comply with more rules and obtain additional licenses that makes 
it more difficult for them to underreport sales. If our results are driven by underreporting of sales, 
we should find that the negative relationship between informal competition and labor productivity 
is smaller (less negative) for firms that export, have foreign owners, bought assets during the past 
year, and are in the capital city. We tested this assumption by interacting all these variables with the 
informal competition variable and adding the interaction term (one by one) to the final specification 
(shown in column 5, Table 4). However, none of these interaction terms were found to be significant.
6 | CONCLUSIONS
Despite the employment opportunities provided by the informal sector, there is concern that high lev-
els of informality can undermine aggregate productivity and retard overall development. Aggregate 
productivity may decline in two ways. First, informal firms tend to be highly unproductive compared 
to the formal sector. Thus, aggregate productivity falls with rising informality. Second, informal com-
petition may hurt the productivity of the formal sector firms, and so higher informality lowers aggre-
gate productivity.
Using firm-level survey data for a cross section of mostly developing countries, we document that 
the labor productivity of informal firms is only about a quarter of the labor productivity of the for -
mal firms. Moreover, we show that the competition from informal firms negatively affects the labor 
productivity of formal firms by about 19%–24%. These findings support the two informality-related 
concerns raised earlier.

Our analysis goes beyond the overall effect. We find that the productivity gap between formal and 
informal firms is larger among formal firms that are better endowed with productivity-enhancing 
factors (e.g., location in the capital city and older firms). Thus, informal firms appear less capable of 
leveraging productivity-enhancing opportunities than formal firms. We also find that informal compe-
tition is more detrimental to the labor productivity of formal firms when the business environment is 
less conducive to operate formally. This suggests that the “unfair” advantage enjoyed by the informal 
firms over the formal firms is exacerbated by a business environment that offers fewer benefits or 
imposes greater costs on the formal firms.
Several issues remain to be explored. First, the factors that cause the productivity gap and the 
channels through which informal competition affects formal firms’ labor productivity need further 
exploration. This is important to sharpen policy responses to the challenges faced by formal and in-
formal firms. Second, the impact of informal competition on formal firms can be extended to other 
performance measures such as sales and employment growth and innovation. Third, the importance 
of other business environment factors—physical infrastructure availability, financial development, 
policies toward SMEs, and tax exemptions—in shaping the productivity gap and the impact of infor -
mal competition on the productivity of formal firms can be explored. We leave these issues for future 
research.
