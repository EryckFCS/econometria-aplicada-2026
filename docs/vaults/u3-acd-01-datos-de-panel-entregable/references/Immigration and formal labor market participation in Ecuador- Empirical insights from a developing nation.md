ABSTRACT  
This research article reports an investigation of immigration effects on the probability of being part of the formal 
labor market in Ecuador, a developing South American economy. We use data on the population of Ecuador as 
collected in the Census of Population and Dwelling 2010. Using a discrete-choice probit model, we find a 
negative association between immigration and the probability of having a job in the formal labor market. This 
relationship stands for both international and local immigrants. We also find that long-term migrants (local or 
international) exhibit an increased likelihood of securing formal employment, while recent migrants face hurdles 
in accessing the formal labor market. In addition, our results show that international migrants face more diffi -
culties in entering the formal labor market compared to local immigrants.   
Introduction 
One of the objectives within the United Nations ’ Sustainable 
Development Goals (SDGs) is to improve access to and conditions of 
employment of the general population ( United Nations, n.d.-a ). Working 
is a daily activity and the primary source of economic resources for most 
human beings and has a profound influence on an individual ’ s and the 
household ’ s perception of their own well-being. Thus, employment is 
the best guarantee of harmonious economic growth and development 
( Comisi ´on Econ ´omica para Am ´erica Latina y el Caribe (CEPAL), 2007; 
International Labour Organization, n.d. ). Indeed, unemployment affects 
citizens at the individual, family, and societal level. At the individual 
level, unemployment causes stress and anxiety ( Gallie, Kostova, & 
Kuchar, 2001 ), increases debt and solvency problems, weakens matri -
monial bonds, and even places children ’ s access to public education at 
risk ( Dixon, 1992 ). At the societal level, unemployment generates 
various problems such as an increase in the level of poverty ( Corcoran & 
Hill, 1980 ). 
The labor market can be divided into two sectors: the formal and 
informal labor markets. The benefits a worker can access depend upon 
the labor market sector in which the worker participates. In 2016, 
globally, about 61 % of all workers participated in the informal market 
( United Nations, n.d.-c ), which should set off alarm bells since the 
informal market is characterized by smaller or fewer economic and so -
cial benefits ( Friberg, 2012; International Labour Office, 2002; Tijdens, 
Besamusca, & Van Klaveren, 2015 ). A lack of job opportunities in the 
formal sector pushes citizens into the informal sector, which offers easier 
access to work ( Banerjee, 1983 ). The job search therefore takes place 
within an environment of competition that fosters inequality between 
social groups — for example, between local people and newcomers — for 
those trying to secure a position in the formal sector, which it is asso -
ciated with a perception of stability and individual well-being. The ways 
in which immigration, both internal and international, is related to the 
formal labor market is the focus of this paper. 
In 2020, around 272 million people lived in a country other than the 
one in which they were born ( United Nations. n.d.-b ). The leading cause 
of migration is the search for better living conditions, an increase in 
income, and job stability. However, it takes time for migrants to insert 
themselves into a local labor market. Migrants show a desire to ensure 
and protect job opportunities but may not consider the structure of the 
labor market ( Godin & Renaud, 2005 ). Two theoretical approaches are 
used to analyze the relationships between migration and the labor 
market. The first suggests that migrants replace people in the native 
workforce, while the second indicates that migrants fill workforce po -
sitions that native workers do not want ( Dustmann, Schonberg, & 
Stuhler, 2016; Gang & Rivera-Batiz, 1994; LaLonde & Topel, 1991 ). 
In the context of a globalized world, understanding the dynamics of 
labor market integration for migrants has become increasingly vital. 
This research aims to contribute to the discourse on migration and labor 
markets, focusing on Ecuador as a case study. The study is particularly

Ecuador is a developing country that has experienced a conspicuous 
urbanization process; in 2021, for example, 64 % of the population 
resided in urban areas ( World Bank, 2022 ). Moreover, between the late 
1990 s and 2000 Ecuador absorbed a massive wave of immigrants. In 
order to classify workers in this country as formal and informal, this 
paper uses the affiliation status of an individual to the country ’ s social 
security system (IESS), as this coverage is required for formal employ -
ment in the country ( IESS, 2022 ). 
At the beginning of 2022, the Ecuadorian labor market was distrib -
uted as follows: 42.5 % worked in the formal sector and 51.4 % in the 
informal sector ( National Institute of Statistics and the Census (INEC), 
2022c ). We use data from the 2010 Census on Population and Housing to 
determine whether there were significant differences in the participa -
tion of internal and foreign migrants in the formal workforce in the year 
2010, taking into account that workers immigrated at different points in 
time. 
The findings of this research confirm that there are substantive dif -
ferences in how migrants — both internal migrants and those coming 
from abroad — insert themselves into the labor market compared to local 
workers. Specifically, the results suggest more recently arrived migrant 
have lower chances of finding formal-market jobs. Interestingly, this 
relationship weakens over time. Our results also indicate that in the case 
of foreign migrants arriving in Ecuador, even if they have been residing 
there for a while, the long-term difficulty of obtaining formal employ -
ment remains.

Section 6. 
Review of the literature 
Labor Markets: Formal and Informal 
Throughout history, the behavior of the labor market has been 
examined from various perspectives. In practical terms, the literature 
usually divides the labor market into two groups. The first is a tradi -
tional, regulated market. Pradhan and van Soest (1995) show that 
workers in this sector usually work under a contract in which benefits 
are laid out according to labor law. Banerjee (1983) and Harris and 
Todaro (1970) point out that this sector offers stable conditions to 
workers with regard to salaries. Similarly, Friberg (2012) mentions that 
access to social security is guaranteed for this sector. These factors make 
formal positions more attractive to workers, even though expectations 
may not always be met.. 
On the other hand, the informal labor market is characterized by jobs 
that lack social security coverage and written labor contracts ( Interna -
tional Labour Office, 2002 ). Tijdens et al. (2015) add that salaries in the 
informal sector are lower than the minimum defined by law and may 
involve workweeks greater than 48 h. Moreover, Sheikh and Gaurav 
(2020) and Friberg (2012) indicate that this sector is not regulated by 
labor law, resulting in minimal benefits and conditions that are not 
favorable to workers. The informal market is therefore the “ second 
choice ” for workers after the formal market. It is an option chosen in the 
face of a lack of sources of formal work, but also, as Banerjee (1983) 
asserts, for the ease with which one can establish oneself in a market 
niche in this sector and enjoy relative freedom. In this regard, the In -
ternational Labour Office (2002) emphasizes the importance of informal 
work to the economy, since in many countries informal work generates 
wealth, employment, and sufficient income to sustain both oneself and a 
family. 
The relationship between these two labor market segments — formal 
and informal — has been extensively explored in the literature. For 
example, Fields (1975) discusses how the informal sector often serves as 
a bridge to the formal sector. Individuals decide to start work in the 
informal sector while they develop business connections or look for 
opportunities in the formal sector. However, Breman (1976) states that 
no such relationship between the sectors exists, and individuals who 
work informally continue in these types of positions, not so much 
because they choose to or due to a lack of education, but rather because 
the sectors have distinct market structures and barriers to entry. 
Dustmann et al. (2016) offer another view on entry into the formal 
and informal labor sectors, pointing out that entry depends on the 
economic status of one ’ s family. That is, if an individual is a member of a 
wealthy family who controls various businesses by inheritance, they can 
quickly enter the formal sector. In addition, if an individual ’ s parents 
have attained some higher education, their children will probably 
receive a good education in which they will network with people who 
can later help them enter the formal sector. On the other hand, for 
groups excluded by xenophobia due to race, migratory status, poverty, 
religion, or other types of discrimination, access to the formal labor 
market will depend on academic merit or experience, or positions ar -
ranged by influential relatives or contacts. 
Mobility and the Labor Market 
The United Nations (1970) , in the framework of the International 
Organization for Migration (IOM) (n.d.) , defines a migrant as a person 
who changes their usual place of residence for another, whether on a 
temporary or permanent basis. The United Nations High Commissioner 
for Refugees (UNHCR) (n.d.) defines the nature of this mobility, differ -
entiating between voluntary and involuntary relocation. That is to say, 
the UNHCR distinguishes a migrant, a person who freely decides to 
change residence, from a refugee. The latter is forced to abandon their 
place of origin due to threats against their life and liberty — their 
displacement is involuntary. 
Moreover, an individual ’ s mobility status can be either national or 
international ( World Bank, 2016; Rees, 2020 ). National, or internal, 
mobility relates to individuals who change their place of residence 
within the same country. In contrast, international movement covers 
those who emigrate to new countries. Banerjee (1983) mentions that, by 
nature, the process of migrating to and integrating into receiver com -
munities and territories implies economic, social, and cultural reorga -
nization processes, both for the destination and the country of origin. 
Authors such as Banerjee (1983), David & Jarreau (2017) , and 
Mintchev et al. (2017) note that if migration is voluntary, the migrant ’ s 
decision is influenced by the conditions of his/her surroundings, such as 
the degree of unemployment, the political and economic situation 
within the country of origin, and others. Subsequently, the migrant does 
a cost-benefit analysis of relocating, taking into consideration the 
opinions of other family members. Usually, in the first stage, only one 
family member migrates, seeking to improve her or his conditions with a 
higher income and stability. Finally, depending upon the living condi -
tions that the migrant encounters in the destination and the connections 
that she or he has made, she or he might be joined by the rest of the 
family or other people familiar to her or him. 
After changing residence, a migrant — whether internal or coming 
from abroad — must decide whether she or he will return to the place of 
origin or will settle down in the new place. This decision depends upon 
various factors such as economic stability, their personal ability to adapt 
(resilience), expectations regarding finding employment, their capacity 
to save money and to establish a family and social connections, and the 
discrimination that they face on a daily basis ( Friberg, 2012 ). 
In the case of forced displacement, refugees have less time to react 
and must usually abandon their place of origin rapidly. Thus, the deci -
sion of where to migrate is influenced by factors such as the number of 
fellow nationals who already reside at the destination ( Neumayer, 2005 )

In relation to migration ’ s effect on the labor market, studies have 
obtained mixed results. On the one hand, one stream of published 
research suggests that migrants replace the work done by native 
workers, while the other faction indicates that migrants occupy job 
positions that native workers reject ( Dustmann et al., 2016; Gang & 
Rivera-Batiz, 1994; LaLonde & Topel, 1991 ). The first implies an 
excessive workforce in the labor market, giving rise to precarity in wages 
( LaLonde & Topel, 1991 ). Moreover, Dustmann et al. (2016) note that 
this condition would create a situation where less well-prepared native 
workers confront migrants, which could generate unemployment for 
both groups. On the other hand, the second strand of literature, which 
proposes that migrants take up positions that native workers have 
refused, would result in benefits to the labor market: As employers are 
inclined to contract personnel immediately available for many activities, 
there would be no barriers to migrant access ( Gang & Rivera-Batiz, 
1994 ). In discussing these situations, it is important to note the condi -
tions under which migrants enter the workforce, that is, whether they 
enter the formal or informal sector. 
Borjas (2006) points out that a country ’ s residents tend to migrate 
when their country starts to accept new arrivals, whether they are in -
ternal or foreign migrants, due to the fact that wherever there is inter -
national migration, the new settlers come to look for work. For some 
native workers, the arrival of newcomers may motivate their decision to 
move to a less competitive environment where they can find the best 
conditions for entry into the labor market. 
Studies such as those by Godin and Renaud (2005) acknowledge a 
strong relationship between migration and insertion into the labor 
market. These authors highlight the importance that people perceived as 
migrants put on assuring and protecting their employment, often 
without regard for the structure of the labor market or the challenges 
they must face. In other words, integrating migrants into the labor 
market is a complex process since, during the job search, migrants 
encounter more significant obstacles than the rest of the receiving 
country ’ s population. Often during this process, one can observe a 
particular segmentation within this integration, given that certain 
groups are not inclined to accept migrants ( Remennick, 2003 ). 
Concerning cross-border migration, immigrant networks (in addition 
to characteristics shared between the destination country and the 
country of origin, in terms of cultural and linguistic aspects) provide 
forms of support for immigrants who would otherwise have labor dis -
advantages. Immigrants are willing to accept lower wages than non- 
migrants and compete with each other for jobs ( Massey, 1990 ). 
Regarding this latter point, we can ask, “ Are there marked differences 
between internal and international migrants in terms of how they insert 
themselves into the workforce? ” 
White and Liang (1998) suggest that internal migrants have better 
chances of finding good jobs when moving to a new area compared to 
their chances back home. However, there is significant individual vari -
ation across internal migrants in terms of labor market outcomes. 
Generally, individuals with lower levels of education and job experience 
tend to migrate to places where there are fewer migrants, to avoid 
competition for employment. More qualified migrants will conduct job 
searches in higher-wage sectors, entering into competition with other 
international migrants and local residents. 
Does integration become easier over the years? It seems that the 
ability of individuals to adapt may lead to stability over the long term. 
However, according to Godin and Renaud (2005) and the results of their 
study on work and migration, migrants still face problems inserting 
themselves into the workforce after ten years of arrival, due to 
discrimination against persons with a migratory status. These authors 
also argue that a migrant ’ s nationality influences their integration into 
the labor market. 
De Brauw et al. (2014) note that internal migration is key to a 
country ’ s economic development. However, when migration flows are 
small and the population continues to grow, there are usually economic 
growth problems. In comparison, significant migration flows are ex -
pected to provide a country with favorable monetary returns that help 
reduce overall poverty. 
Given this background in the literature, the current study aims to 
investigate the effects of internal and international migration on the 
probability to be part of the formal labor sector in Ecuador. First, we 
look at long-term prospects, in which it is hoped that both migrant 
groups will have better opportunities to access formal labor. We then 
focus on short-term prospects, taking as our reference point data from 
the last five years of the 2010 Population and Housing Census in 
Ecuador. In the short term, we expect migrant workers to experience 
more significant challenges accessing positions in the formal sector 
compared to the informal sector.

The Ecuadorian Labor Market 
To understand the labor market in Ecuador and its relationship to 
internal migration, it is necessary to understand the structure of that 
market. Fig. 1 shows the structure according to data from INEC (2021) . 
The Ecuadorian statistical agency subdivides employment into three 
categories: adequate, inadequate, and undefined. Adequate employment 
refers to people who work 40 or more hours per week and who, during 
the previous month, had an income equal to or greater than the mini -
mum wage, regardless of whether they worked additional hours or their 
availability to do so. This category also includes people who work less 
than 40 h per week but who, during the previous month, earned monthly 
wages equal to or higher than the minimum wage but did not work 
additional hours ( INEC, 2021 ). In contrast, inadequate employment 
does not satisfy the minimal conditions for hours or wages. Workers with 
inadequate employment constitute the bulk of people classified as being 
underemployed, having other inadequate types of employment, and 
having non-remunerated employment ( INEC, 2021 ). Undefined 
employment refers to the remaining workers ( INEC, 2021 ). The defini -
tions that INEC offers regarding employment do not take into account 
the distinction between formal and informal work and only consider 
factors such as the number of hours worked weekly or the minimal 
salary. They do not consider factors such as labor stability, social secu -
rity, and others. Another category that takes into account all these fac -
tors is known as decent employment or good work ( INEC, 2015b ).

Migratory Flows Within, to, and From Ecuador 
We can divide migration in Ecuador into various stages. The first 
stage occurred between 1960 and 1980 and was chiefly marked by in -
ternal migration. Authors such as Bravo-Ureta et al. (1996) and Rudel 
(1983) point out that a main migratory flow in this period was toward 
Ecuador ’ s Amazonian provinces,
b 
which were sparsely populated at this 
time. This migration was mainly of a rural-rural or urban – rural nature. 
The migrants were from groups with medium to high levels of pur -
chasing power; that is, they sought to expand their property holdings by 
obtaining property in the Amazon. The petroleum production boom 
occurring during this period made this region particularly attractive to 
migrants ( Cebri ´an, 1999 ). 
Bride (1971) has shown that during this period, Ecuador was also 
experiencing a notable urbanization process, however, with the coastal 
provinces absorbing significant numbers of migrants motivated by op -
portunities offered by burgeoning export activities. Guayas and El Oro, 
located in Ecuador ’ s coastal region, and Pichincha, located in the An -
dean region where Ecuador ’ s capital, Quito, is also located, were the 
provinces perceived as particularly attractive to migrants, even up until 
2001 ( Pontarollo & Segovia, 2019 ). 
In 1984, the number of people living in urban areas surpassed the 
rural population, and in 2021 urban residents comprised 64.36 % of the 
total population, as indicated in Fig. 2 . This trend of urbanization trig -
gered other cumulative effects in specific economic and demographic 
sectors ( Correa, 2016 ). According to Alvarado L ´opez et al. (2017) the 
most attractive sectors — provinces — to migrants are those that are hubs 
of significant economic activity with businesses, production centers, 
tourist centers, as migrants tend to originate from provinces with less 
economic activity. Indeed, authors such as Royuela and Ord ´o ˜nez (2016) 
indicate that migrants are concentrated in the two provinces that 
contain the country ’ s main cities, Quito and Guayaquil. However, these 
authors also note that the trend has weakened, to the extent that the 
provinces with the greatest influx of recent migrants are not necessarily 
the most populated. 
Beginning in 1900, Ecuador experienced the most jolting crisis in its 
history, characterized by an elevated level of poverty, high inflation, 
high levels of unemployment, restrictions on liquidity, and a deterio -
ration of living conditions ( Acosta et al., 2005; Bertoli, Fern ´andez- 
Huertas Moraga, & Ortega, 2011 ). These factors compelled many 
Ecuadorians to migrate abroad ( Bertoli & Marchetta, 2014 ), with Spain, 
the USA, and Italy being the preferred destinations ( Sotomayor, Barrios, 
& Chininin, 2019 ). 
Overseas emigration again became common in the late 1990 s. Be -
tween 1998 and 2001, more than half a million Ecuadorians migrated 
out of the country ( Bertoli, Fern ´andez-Huertas Moraga, & Ortega, 2011 ). 
Villamar (2004) notes that most migrants were from the provinces of 
Azuay and Ca ˜nar, located in the highland region. 
In 2000, Ecuador ’ s transition to the US dollar coupled with re -
mittances from the new emigrants allowed the country to regain a sense 
of economic and political stability, thus reducing levels of emigration 
( Acosta et al., 2005 ). The reduction in migratory flows can be seen in

Discussion and conclusion 
We analyzed the effects of human migration on the labor market. 
Specifically, we tried to verify whether there are significant differences 
in how internal and international migrants insert themselves into the 
workforce. To achieve this, we applied a probit binary choice model to 
determine the factors influencing the probability of obtaining a position 
in the formal labor sector, using data from the 2010 Population and 
Housing Census in Ecuador. We used the whole population for the 
empirical analysis, ensuring the robustness of our results. 
We found that regardless of whether a migrant is internal or foreign, 
or whether a person lives and works in a place distinct from their place 
of birth, this person has a higher probability of securing a job in the 
formal sector compared to non-migrants. In addition, recent migrants, 
that is, those who were living in a place other than their current place of 
residence five years prior, have a lower probability of finding work in 
the formal sector than non-migrants. These findings conform to results 
from prior studies that show that there are different work inclusion is not 
homogeneous and is influenced by factors such as one ’ s ability to adapt 
to new surroundings, personal networks, and discrimination ( Arendt 
et al., 2016; White & Liang, 1998 ). 
Another interesting result that our investigation uncovered is related 
to the foreign migrant ’ s ability to enter the formal labor market. Our 
empirical study reveals that over both the short and long term, the dif -
ficulties migrants face in accessing formal employment persist, that is, 
the foreign migrant community has a lower probability of obtaining 
work in the formal sector compared to those born in Ecuador. As years of 
residence in Ecuador increase, the probability of obtaining formal work 
will depend on how well a migrant adapts to the new environment and 
how extensive the migrant ’ s network of contacts is. Our findings 
corroborate those of Godin and Renaud (2005) and Valenzuela et al. 
(2018) , who identify difficulties that migrant communities that emigrate 
to developing countries encounter when they try to enter the formal 
sector, probably due to discrimination and xenophobia, or to a general 
lack of opportunities available to foreigners. 
From the viewpoint of public policy and knowing the potential that 
migration represents in recipient economies, the government should 
prioritize programs of rapid labor incorporation for migrants (both in -
ternal and foreign), preferably in the formal market. For example, pro -
fessional training in areas that face labor shortages or local language 
courses for non-Spanish-speaking foreigners could help migrants over -
come some of the obstacles they encounter in trying to enter the formal 
labor market. In addition, in the case of skilled workers, it would be 
helpful to facilitate the recognition and standardization of degrees for 
foreign migrants; estimates indicate that 73 % of migrants reside in 
Ecuador without the possibility of practicing their profession legally 
( GTRM & R4V, 2022 ). Clearly, if a migrant lacks the necessary docu -
mentation, they cannot bid for jobs in the formal market, nor can they 
quickly set up their own formal business. 
The migration process has many repercussions for both source and 
destination countries. For example, one of the significant repercussions 
in receiving countries is the accelerated urbanization that migration 
triggers. Governments need to formulate plans that identify anticipated 
migratory flows in receiving zones and establish systems that offer 
essential services, connectivity (transportation), access to public health 
and education, etc., in those areas, so as to effectively manage urban 
development. Moreover, given that half the total workforce in Ecuador 
works in the informal sector, which consists primarily of small busi -
nesses with little value added, policies should focus on distributing re -
sources that extend the lifetimes of these businesses, as well as support 
their expansion and formalization. 
Fruitful avenues for future research in this area include differenti -
ating between different types of migrant flows — for example, voluntary 
vs. refugee migration — since this may affect the ability of migrants to 
insert themselves into the labor market. In addition, the relationship 
between the labor market and generational differences among migrants 
may provide interesting insights into the labor market outcomes of 
second- and third-generation migrants, as according to Neilsen et al. 
(2003) , the children of migrants still face problems in terms of access to 
the formal labor market.
